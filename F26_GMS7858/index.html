An Individualized Bayesian Method for Estimating Genomic Variants of Hypertension

The Problem

Hypertension

  • Hypertension is the leading modifiable risk factor for cardiovascular disease
  • ~7.8 million deaths worldwide in 2015 were attributable to high blood pressure
  • Hypertension is a polygenic disease that is driven by many genomic variants, not one
  • Genomic risk is only one factor, along with lifestyle, environment, and other comorbidities, but this paper focuses primarily on the genomic components

Approaches used to find genomic risk variants

  • The state of the art method of identifying genomic risk variants is Genome-Wide Association Studies (GWAS)

  • GWAS identify variant-trait associations at a population level, testing each SNP via Logistic regression or Fisher’s exact test with genome-wide signficance at p \(< 5 x 10^{-8}\)

  • GWAS takes a large population, split into cases vs. controls

  • Visualization is commonly done with a Manhattan plot

Limitations of GWAS

  • Needs huge sample sizes (~1 million people) to detect low-frequency variants especially those with low minor allele frequency (MAF)
  • The ~1,000 known BP loci explain only ~5.7% of the genetic variance in blood pressure
  • Most detected SNPs are common, small-effect variants
  • GWAS can answer questions like what is the population-level risk of a particular variant? but it cannot answer questions like what is the risk of a particular variant for a specific patient?

Important

A given hypertensive patient may carry none of the population’s significant risk alleles

Individualized Bayesian Inference (IBI)

The Core Idea

Instead of researching “what matters for the average patient?”, the goal is to observe “what matters for patients who share this specific variant with me?”

  • IBI models each variant’s effect in the subpopulation that actually carries it
  • Builds on TCI (tumor-specific causal inference), originally developed for individual cancer genomes
  • IBI aims to identify personalized, potentially causal genomic drivers of a trait, beyond just population-average associations

Building Block: Bayesian Networks

  • IBI uses a bipartite Bayesian Network (BN) with two node sets-

    • Set 1: Variants \(V\) (e.g., SNPs)
    • Set 2: Traits \(T\) (e.g., hypertension)

  • Directed edges \(V -> T\) represent probabilistic dependency with only one assumed influencing variant being evaluated at a time indicated by solid arc

  • Network structures are scored using the Bayesian Dirichlet equivalent uniform (BDeu) score that tells you how well a proposed structure is supported by the data

Population-level Bayesian Scoring using GWAS

  • For variant \(V_s\) and trait \(T_i\) over the full population \(D\), the marginal likelihood \(P(D \mid V_s \to T_i)\) (log form: \(M_s\)) is computed

  • Under an uniform prior, ranking by \(M_s\) is proportional to ranking by global posterior probability, and is directly comparable to GWAS’s p-value ranking

The Individualizing Step

For a given patient \(h\) who carries the minor allele of

\(V_s\) (\(V_s = 1\))

  1. Partition the population into \(D^{V_s=1}\) (“patients-like-me,” carrying the variant) and \(D^{V_s=0}\) (everyone else)
  2. Score \(V_s\)’s ability to explain Hypertension within \(D^{V_s=1}\) -> \(M_s^1\)
  3. Find the variant \(V_r\) that best explains the remaining group \(D^{V_s=0}\) -> \(M_r^0\)
  4. Combine: \(M_{s,r} = M_s^1 + M_r^0\) This overall score, \(M_{s,r}\), is higher than the single-population estimator’s score (\(M_s\) using one global variant \(V_g\)) for many SNPs, because two distinct, subgroup-specific explanations together can fit the data better than one global explanation

One variant explains “people like this patient.” A second explains everyone else. Together they almost always beat the single best population-wide variant

Personalized posterior probability

  • For each patient, \(M_{s,r}\) is normalized across only the minor-allele SNPs present in that individual’s genome, yielding an individualized posterior probability \(P(V_s^h \to T_i^h \mid D)\)

  • The same SNP can therefore rank differently across patients depending on their distinct genomic backgrounds

  • The highest-ranked variant for a patient is IBI’s proposed individualized explanation of their HTN status

Applying IBI to Real Data

The Cohort: Framingham Heart Study

  • Cohort: Framingham Heart Study (FHS), Affymetrix HuGeneFocused50K genotyping array (dbGaP phs000007.v30.p11)
  • QC: --mind 0.03 --geno 0.03 --maf 0.01 --hwe 10e-6 --me 0.05 0.1 --sexCheck gives 38,342 SNPs
    • After removing minor-allele risk ratio < 1, final count= 19,276 SNPs
  • Linkage Disequilibrium (LD) pruning: d′ threshold 0.2 (SNPRelate) reduces it to 19,006 SNPs (98.5% independent)
  • Final analytic sample: 6,613 patients
  • Split: 80% discovery (5,290) / 20% held-out test (1,323)

Defining “Hypertension”

A patient is coded HTN = 1 if:

  • Systolic BP ≥ 140 mmHg, or
  • Diastolic BP ≥ 90 mmHg, or
  • Currently on antihypertensive medication (+10 mmHg SBP / +5 mmHg DBP correction applied if on treatment, per TOPMed harmonization)

Results

Comparison

Fig 2. A Bayesian method for GWAS analysis

  • Population-level \(M_s\) and GWAS p-values are highly concordant

  • Spearman ρ = −0.90; 164/189 top independent SNPs and 18/20 top-5-ranked SNPs overlap between rankings

  • This establishes that the Bayesian framework reproduces GWAS when used non-individually, before the individualizing extension is applied

IBI vs. GWAS: variant discovery (discovery set, n=5,290)

  • GWAS (Bonferroni α = 0.05/19,276 = 2.59×10⁻⁶): 5 SNPs reach significance
  • IBI: 189 SNPs exceed the population-level best-estimator’s \(M_s\) (i.e., a two-subgroup explanation beats the single best global explanation)
  • Overlap: 40 of the top-189 SNPs shared between IBI and GWAS (agreement); 148 unique to one or the other (complementarity)
  • MAF distribution: IBI-only SNPs have markedly lower MAF than GWAS-only SNPs. IBI’s individualized partitioning better surfaces rare-variant signal
  • Information gain (IG): IG for IBI’s top-189 SNPs > GWAS’s top-189 SNPs > 189 random SNPs (all comparisons directionally consistent with expectation)

Patient-Level Explanation

GWAS, using population-significant SNPs

  • Only 16 unique SNPs ever serve as a patient’s top explanatory variant
  • Only 12.9% of HTN patients carry a Bonferroni-significant SNP
  • Even with relaxed threshold, only 15.7% covered

IBI

  • 25 unique SNPs serve as top-1 explanatory variants (more diverse coverage)
  • 99.1% of HTN patients get a top SNP with posterior probability ≥ 0.10
  • 79.7% still covered at a stricter threshold of 0.20
  • Average patient carries 7,767 minor-allele SNPs (of 19,276)
  • Under a naive uniform prior this implies ~1.3×10⁻⁴ per-SNP prior probability
  • IBI’s assigned posterior probabilities (0.08–0.99 range for top SNP) are far above this baseline
  • That’s the clinical gap this paper is trying to close — most hypertensive patients simply don’t carry a “significant” population-level variant

Spotlight: One SNP, 425 Patients

  • Intronic SNP rs13265032 in the CSMD1 locus
  • Assigned as the top explanatory variant for 46% (425/930) of HTN patients by IBI
  • Ranked 12th by IBI’s own scoring which implies a real signal, not noise
  • Ranked 5,361st by GWAS — essentially invisible to standard analysis
  • CSMD1 variants have been independently linked to BP and hydrochlorothiazide response in prior literature

Strengths of the Study

  • Direct, apples-to-apples comparison against GWAS in the same cohort
  • Multiple independent lines of evidence: overlap analysis, information gain, MAF distribution, literature cross-checks, held-out prediction
  • Fully reproducible: code and summary data are public on GitHub
  • Grounded in a well-characterized, real longitudinal cohort (Framingham)

Questions & Limitations

  • Does not yet adjust for age, sex, population structure, or family relatedness (planned: linear mixed models)
  • Associations are not necessarily causal, they are confounded by LD and population structure
  • AUROC improvement (0.55 → 0.59–0.60) is real but modest
  • Single cohort (FHS), genotyping array rather than whole-genome sequencing
  • “Patients-like-me” subgroup size can be very small for rare variants and stability of these estimates is worth scrutinizing

Next steps

  • Extend to whole-genome sequencing data (rare variants are IBI’s sweet spot)
  • Generalize beyond discrete traits to continuous traits (swap BDeu → BIC score) — e.g., lipid levels, continuous BP
  • Incorporate linear mixed models for population structure / relatedness
  • Search for combinations of variants acting jointly, not just one at a time
  • Fine-mapping / functional validation / Mendelian randomization to move from association toward causation

Key Messages

  1. GWAS finds what matters on average — not necessarily what matters for the patient in front of you
  2. IBI reframes variant discovery around the individual’s genomic background, by scoring a “patients-like-me” subgroup separately from everyone else
  3. In the Framingham cohort, IBI explained far more hypertensive patients (99% vs. 16%), surfaced biologically plausible rare variants GWAS missed, and gave a modest but significant boost in predictive accuracy

Glossary of Concepts

Bayesian Networks & Bayesian Inference

Bayesian network: a directed acyclic graph (DAG) where nodes are random variables and edges encode probabilistic (and candidate causal) dependencies. The joint distribution factorizes along the graph

Bayesian inference: updating a prior belief about a hypothesis with observed data (via the likelihood) to get a posterior belief using Bayes’ rule

Marginal Likelihood & Bayesian Scoring

  • Marginal likelihood \(P(D \mid \text{model})\): the probability of the observed data, integrating out uncertainty in the model’s parameters. This is used to compare candidate network structures without overfitting to one parameter setting
  • BDeu score (Bayesian Dirichlet equivalent uniform): a closed-form marginal likelihood for discrete variables with a uniform structural prior. In this study, IBI uses BDeu for binary HTN status
  • BIC (Bayesian information criterion): a large-sample approximation to marginal likelihood, applicable to continuous variables. Under future work, the authors’ proposed swap-in for continuous traits like lipid levels would need to use BIC

Individualized Causal Inference

A family of methods that estimate a model specific to one instance (a patient, a tumor) rather than a single model averaged over the whole population — by partitioning the population into an instance-relevant subgroup and its complement, then combining subgroup-specific explanations.

DAGs & the Causal Markov Condition

Directed acyclic graph (DAG):

  • nodes = variables
  • directed edges = direct dependence, no directed cycles

Causal Markov condition:

  • Each node is independent of its non-descendants, given its direct parents. This lets the joint distribution factorize into local pieces

IBI’s own graph is deliberately minimal and it is designed as bipartite, and only one edge \(V_s \to T_i\) evaluated at a time. It never learns a structure with multiple parents pointing into \(T\)

Faithfulness — and IBI’s Consistency Claim

Faithfulness: every conditional independence present in the data-generating distribution is a consequence of the graph’s structure (via d-separation) and is not a coincidental cancellation of two or more paths’ effects

  • Faithfulness is what makes learning causal structure from observational data alone possible at all
  • Not explicitly tested in this paper: Genomic data is a plausible place for near-violations: strong linkage disequilibrium can produce highly correlated or near-cancelling signals between nearby SNPs, where faithfulness condition may not hold

Confounding: LD & Population Structure

Confounding: a third variable that influences both the “exposure” (a SNP) and the “outcome” (HTN), creating a spurious or inflated association

Linkage disequilibrium (LD): nearby SNPs are correlated, so a flagged SNP may just be “riding along” with the true causal variant

Population structure: ancestry differences can independently affect both allele frequencies and disease prevalence, inflating spurious associations — this is exactly why the authors flag it as unmodeled in the current IBI implementation

Hypertension causal structure is more complex than IBI’s Arc

IBI evaluates a single arc \(V_s \to T_i\). The true system generating hypertension almost certainly has more structure behind it, including

LD / haplotype fork: SNPs that operate close together are generally inherited together on the same ancestral chromosome segment

  • A latent haplotype block \(H\) can be a common cause of two correlated SNPs
  • One or more SNPs may just “ride along” with the true causal variant

Collider risk in case-control conditioning:

  • Patient-Coverage results analyze only within the Hypertension patients hence bringing means implicitly conditioning on the trait

  • If two variants each independently influence HTN, that would be a collider, and conditioning on HTN status can induce a spurious association between them that wouldn’t exist population-wide

Information Gain

Information gain (IG): the reduction in uncertainty (entropy) about the outcome (HTN status) once you know a variant’s genotype

\[IG(T; V) = H(T) - H(T \mid V)\] - \(H(T)\): entropy of HTN status before knowing anything about the variant. - \(H(T \mid V)\): remaining entropy after conditioning on the variant’s genotype

  • A variant that perfectly separates cases from controls has high IG; a variant unrelated to HTN has IG ≈ 0
  • Used in the paper as an independent check that IBI-selected SNPs are more informative about HTN than GWAS-selected or random SNPs
