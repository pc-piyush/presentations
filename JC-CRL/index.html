Piyush Chaudhari

Journal Club

2026-11-16

Paper at a Glance

Core idea:

  • Causality research usually assumes the causal variables are given

  • Deep learning learns representations but ignores causality

  • The central open problem is causal representation learning: discovering high-level causal variables from low-level observations such as pixels.

Three tasks in one paper:

  1. A primer on causal inference for ML readers (Structured Causal Models (SCMs), interventions, counterfactuals)

  2. The Independent Causal Mechanisms (ICM) principle and the Sparse Mechanism Shift (SMS) hypothesis as inductive biases

  3. What a causal view implies for semi-supervised learning, robustness, adversarial examples, RL, and science

Motivation

Modern ML wins on large-scale pattern recognition when train and test are drawn from the same distribution. Outside that setting:

Issue 1: Robustness

  • Accuracy collapses under blur, noise, rotation, new viewpoints or backgrounds
  • Augmentation, pre-training and self-supervision help, but only by widening the training distribution
  • The authors argue that true out-of-distribution generalization needs a model where shifts are interventions on a causal system

Issue 2: Reusable mechanisms

  • Animals don’t relearn physics for every new task
  • If a model’s modules correspond to physical mechanisms, a new environment should only require adapting a few of them
  • Today’s networks are trained end-to-end and re-learned as a whole

Interventions, domain shifts and temporal structure are what animals learn from; standard ML treats them as nuisances to average away

Why Conditional Probabilities Aren’t Enough

The classic example: across European regions, stork populations correlate with human birth rates

A statistical model can exploit this

  • \(P(\text{births} \mid \text{storks})\) is genuinely predictive in the i.i.d. setting
  • Nothing is “wrong” with the prediction as long as nothing changes

But it can’t answer intervention questions

  • “If we released more storks, would births rise?”
  • That asks for \(P(\text{births} \mid do(\text{storks}))\), which observational conditionals alone can’t supply

Central claim:

  • a conditional distribution tells you what to expect when you observe something;

  • a causal model tells you what happens when you act

  • Generalizing under shift needs the latter.

The Gold Standard: Physical Models

A system of differential equations with initial condition \(\mathbf{x}(t_0)=\mathbf{x}_0\):

\[\frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt}=f(\mathbf{x}),\qquad \mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\]

Discretized, it reads as a causal update rule: the future is computed from the present

\[\mathbf{x}(t+dt)=\mathbf{x}(t)+dt\cdot f(\mathbf{x}(t))\]

Why it’s the gold standard

  • Predicts under any intervention (change a parameter, re-integrate)
  • Causal structure can be read off \(f\): which components drive which

Why we can’t always use it

  • Needs hand-derived mechanisms and known variables
  • Breaks with unobserved confounders or a coarse-graining that isn’t causal
  • Rarely learnable directly from raw data

Four Things We Might Want From a Model

A. Predict in the i.i.d. setting

Estimate \(P(Y \mid X)\) well. Statistical models suffice; correlations like storks–births are fine here

B. Predict under distribution shift

Interventions change the joint distribution, so i.i.d. guarantees no longer hold. Trust means predictions survive changed conditions

C. Answer counterfactuals

Interventional and counterfactual queries form a continuum, \(P(A \mid B, do(C))\); a counterfactual is the case where \(B\) pins down all noise terms. RL Q-functions sit in between

D. Learn from the data we have

Two axes: observational vs. interventional and hand-engineered vs. raw. Statistical models learn from raw observational data; causal discovery usually needs multiple environments or interventions

Causal Models and Inference

Reichenbach’s Common Cause Principle

If \(X\) and \(Y\) are statistically dependent, there is some \(Z\) that causally influences both and screens off the dependence (\(X \perp\!\!\!\perp Y \mid Z\)). \(Z\) may coincide with \(X\) or \(Y\).

Observational data alone cannot tell these three apart. They imply the same dependence between \(X\) and \(Y\). Distinguishing them needs extra assumptions, interventions, or data from several environments

Structural Causal Models (SCMs)

Each variable is set by a deterministic function of its parents and an independent noise term:

\[X_i := f_i(\mathbf{PA}_i,\, U_i), \qquad i = 1,\dots,n, \qquad U_1,\dots,U_n \text{ jointly independent}\]

Causal (disentangled) factorization

\[P(X_1,\dots,X_n)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}P(X_i \mid \mathbf{PA}_i)\]

The only factorization whose terms match the structural assignments. Each factor is one mechanism

Entangled factorization

\[P(X_1,\dots,X_n)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}P(X_i \mid X_{i+1},\dots,X_n)\]

Equally valid as a description of the joint, but its terms mix several mechanisms together

Both factorizations describe the same observational distribution. The difference only shows up when something changes, and that is exactly the paper’s lever

Interventions: One Model, Many Distributions

Hard

Set \(X_i\) to a constant: cut its incoming edges, fix the value, sample the children

Soft

Change the mechanism \(f_i\) or the noise distribution, but keep the parents

Uncertain

We know something was intervened on, but not which mechanism

Graphical Models vs. SCMs

A graphical model isn’t automatically causal

  • \(X_1 \rightarrow X_2 \rightarrow X_3\) and \(X_1 \leftarrow X_2 \leftarrow X_3\) encode the same conditional independencies
  • They’re Markov equivalent: from observational data alone, the arrow directions are arbitrary
  • A graph becomes causal only when its arrows are claimed to hold under intervention

What each causal object can compute

Interventions Counterfactuals
Causal graphical model yes no
SCM yes yes

The SCM’s explicit noise terms \(U_i\) are what make counterfactuals possible: fix the noise inferred from what happened, then change the intervention

Confounders (unobserved common causes) can create spurious association or mask a real one, which is why many causal discovery methods fail silently in practice

Independent Causal Mechanisms (ICM)

The ICM Principle

Independent Causal Mechanisms: a system’s causal generative process consists of “autonomous modules that do not inform or influence each other.”

The principle carries two separate claims:

(a) No influence

Changing (intervening on) one mechanism \(P(X_i \mid \mathbf{PA}_i)\) leaves every other mechanism unchanged

→ modularity, autonomy, invariance, separate intervenability

(b) No information

Knowing the other mechanisms tells you nothing about a given mechanism \(P(X_i \mid \mathbf{PA}_i)\)

→ in algorithmic terms, the mechanisms’ descriptions share no mutual algorithmic information

Roots go back decades in econometrics and philosophy of science

Example: Altitude and Temperature

Weather-station data: altitude \(A\) causes average annual temperature \(T\)

Causal factorization

\[P(A, T) = \underbrace{P(A)}_{\text{changes by country}}\; \underbrace{P(T \mid A)}_{\text{physics: invariant}}\]

Move from Austria to Switzerland: the distribution of station altitudes changes, but the physical mechanism linking altitude to temperature does not

Anticausal factorization

\[P(A, T) = P(T)\; P(A \mid T)\]

Change the country and both factors shift, because each one mixes geography with physics

The causal factorization transfers: only one module needs re-estimating. This is the practical payoff of ICM for domain adaptation

The Sparse Mechanism Shift (SMS) Hypothesis

SMS: small distribution changes tend to act sparsely or locally in the causal (disentangled) factorization. They rarely affect all factors at once

In the causal factorization

  • A real-world change (new hospital, new camera, new country) touches one or a few mechanisms
  • Most factors \(P(X_i \mid \mathbf{PA}_i)\) stay fixed
  • So the change is cheap to adapt to

In an entangled factorization

  • The same change spreads across many terms
  • Every factor appears to move
  • Adaptation looks expensive and unstructured

Turned around, this is a learning signal: if the “right” representation makes shifts sparse, we can search for representations under which observed shifts are sparse. SMS becomes supervision

Causal Discovery Meets ML

Conditional-independence route

  • Under faithfulness, CI tests recover the graph up to its Markov equivalence class (same skeleton, same “immoralities”)
  • CI testing is hard with finite data and continuous, high-dimensional conditioning sets
  • With only two variables there are no non-trivial CI statements at all

Restricting the function class

General SCM for \(X \rightarrow Y\): \(\;X := U,\; Y := f(X, V)\)

Additive noise model:

\[Y := f(X) + V, \qquad X \perp\!\!\!\perp V\]

Generically, this model cannot be fit in the reverse direction, which breaks the cause–effect symmetry. The exception is the linear-Gaussian case

Multiple environments help: shifts across environments, interventions, or non-stationary data reveal structure, because under SMS the causal mechanisms stay invariant while other things move

Causal Representation Learning (CRL)

The Setting: Causal Variables Aren’t Given

High-dimensional perceptual data as an entangled view of an unknown causal system

Classical causal inference starts from a table of well-defined variables. Real inputs are pixels, waveforms, clinical notes. Someone or something must first decide what the variables are

Problem Formulation

Observations are generated from a small number of latent causal variables through a nonlinear map:

\[X = G(S_1,\dots,S_n), \qquad S_i := f_i(\mathbf{PA}_i, U_i), \qquad P(S_1,\dots,S_n)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}P(S_i \mid \mathbf{PA}_i)\]

  • Train so that \(p \circ f \circ q \approx \mathrm{id}\), an autoencoder with a structural causal model in the bottleneck
  • Recovering the true \(U\) / \(S\) is ill-posed without further assumptions (the same identifiability wall that limits unsupervised disentanglement)

SMS in Pixel Space

One finger moves and the object falls

In pixels: the change is dense. Thousands of values move, entangled together

In the causal model: the change is sparse. One variable is intervened on; its children follow

Three Problems for Causal Representation Learning

1. Learning disentangled representations

Find an encoder whose latents behave like independent causal mechanisms: factorize as \(\prod_i P(S_i \mid \mathbf{PA}_i)\) and respond sparsely to shifts

SMS and multi-environment data supply the missing supervision

2. Learning transferable mechanisms

With limited data and compute, reuse modules that mirror the modularity of the world

A mechanism learned in one task (e.g., how lighting affects appearance) should plug into the next

3. Learning interventional world models & reasoning

Representations should support acting: intervention, planning, and counterfactual “what if I had…?”

Statistical dependence alone isn’t enough; this is the link to thinking as acting in an imagined space

Implications for Machine Learning

Semi-Supervised Learning: Direction Matters

Causal direction: \(X \rightarrow Y\)

\[P(X, Y) = P(X)\,P(Y \mid X)\]

By ICM, \(P(X)\) carries no information about \(P(Y \mid X)\), so unlabeled \(X\) shouldn’t help

Example: predicting a protein from its mRNA sequence

Anticausal direction: \(Y \rightarrow X\)

\[P(X, Y) = P(Y)\,P(X \mid Y)\]

Now \(P(X)\) and \(P(Y \mid X)\) are coupled, so unlabeled \(X\) can help (cluster and low-density assumptions apply)

Example: classifying a digit from its image (the class causes the pixels)

Adversarial Examples and Robustness

Adversarial vulnerability

  • Adversarial examples are a deliberate violation of i.i.d.: tiny pixel changes, huge prediction changes
  • A classifier in the anticausal direction ignores how images are generated
  • Models that approximate the causal generative process should be harder to fool
  • Proposed defense: analysis by synthesis, i.e., check whether the input is consistent with a generative model of the predicted class

Out-of-distribution robustness

Risk in environment \(e\):

\[R^{P^e}(f)=\mathbb{E}_{(X,Y)\sim P^e}\big[\ell(f(X), Y)\big]\]

Robust predictor over the set of environments \(\mathcal{E}\):

\[f^\star = \arg\min_{f}\; \max_{e \in \mathcal{E}}\; R^{P^e}(f)\]

If \(\mathcal{E}\) is generated by interventions on non-target variables, the causal predictor solves this min-max

We never observe every \(e \in \mathcal{E}\), so estimation error on unseen environments can be large. Causal structure is what lets us extrapolate

Applying a Causal Lens

Pre-training

Approximates the min-max by covering a broader training distribution. More environments, implicitly

Data augmentation

Encodes assumed invariances (rotation, crop, colour) as simulated interventions on nuisance variables

Self-supervision

Pretext tasks learn about \(P(X)\), which helps most in anticausal settings

These are heuristic approximations to robust causal learning. The paper’s request: understand which intervention each trick protects against, rather than stacking them blindly

Reinforcement learning is arguably closer to causality: on-policy learning estimates interventional quantities by acting. Off-policy and offline RL reintroduce confounding, and world models and counterfactual reasoning are open frontiers

Science, Medicine and Multi-Task Learning

Scientific applications

  • Personalized medicine: observational clinical data can mislead treatment-effect estimates
  • Covid-19: a Simpson’s paradox in case-fatality rates resolved by causal reasoning about age structure
  • Exoplanets: a causal “half-sibling” model removed shared instrument noise from Kepler data, yielding 36 candidates, 21 later validated
  • Physics simulation and learning dynamics from data

Multi-task & continual learning

  • Humans reuse one generative model of the world across many tasks
  • A shared causal model with SMS-compliant components would let a system:
    • adapt to a new task by changing a few modules
    • learn new mechanisms without forgetting old ones
  • Contrast: a giant end-to-end model must re-fit everything

Open Problems Named by the Authors

1. Non-linear causal relations at scale

When are they learnable? Which training frameworks scale? Do causal modules actually transfer better than non-causal ones?

2. Learning causal variables

Disentangled representations are fixed-size vectors with arbitrary ordering; they can’t handle a changing number of objects. Need modular, task-dependent representations and identifiability theory

3. Biases of existing deep learning

Scale, augmentation and self-supervision all improve robustness, but their effects are tangled. Test methods against specific interventions to build a taxonomy of inductive biases

4. Causally correct models of world and agent

Real RL rarely has clean abstract states. Derive causal variables and graphs from pixels, and model both agent and environment for model-based RL

Strengths and Limitations

Strengths

  • Unifies two communities with a shared vocabulary (SCMs ↔︎ representations)
  • ICM / SMS give concrete, testable inductive biases rather than vague appeals to “causality”
  • Re-interprets familiar phenomena (SSL, adversarial examples, augmentation) with real explanatory power
  • Agenda-setting: much of the later identifiable-CRL literature traces back here

Limitations

  • A position paper: no new experiments or benchmarks
  • Identifiability is acknowledged but not solved; without supervision, “causal variables” may be unrecoverable
  • SMS is a hypothesis; when do real shifts actually look sparse?
  • Little guidance on evaluation: how would we know a learned variable is causal?
  • Scaling claims predate the foundation-model era; does scale already buy much of the promised robustness?

Why This Matters for Clinical Data

Distribution shift is the default

  • Multi-site networks (e.g., PCORnet, OMOP-based federations) are many environments: different populations, coding practices, EHR vendors
  • Under SMS, a new site should change a few mechanisms (case mix, documentation habits), not the underlying physiology
  • Robust models should separate those

Variables aren’t given here either

  • Phenotypes, disease stages, and severity are latent variables we define from codes, labs, and notes
  • Phenotyping and sub-phenotyping are, in effect, representation learning problems
  • Question: which of our learned phenotypes would survive an intervention?

In our own pipelines, which “variables” are we treating as given that are really learned and possibly not causal?

Key Takeaways

1. Statistical models answer “what will I observe?”; causal models answer “what happens if I act?” Generalizing under shift needs the second

2. The ICM principle says mechanisms are modular and independent; the SMS hypothesis says real-world changes touch only a few of them

3. Causal inference assumes the variables are given; causal representation learning is about learning them from raw data, using SMS and multi-environment data as supervision

4. Familiar ML phenomena (SSL working only sometimes, adversarial fragility, the value of augmentation) look less mysterious through a causal lens
