0. The paper in one paragraph

The idea. Causal inference and deep learning have grown up separately. Causal inference has rigorous tools for reasoning about interventions and counterfactuals, but it assumes the variables (smoking, cancer, dose, blood pressure) are already defined and measured. Deep learning is very good at turning raw, high-dimensional data (pixels, audio, text) into useful representations, but those representations are purely statistical and carry no notion of cause and effect. The paper argues that the central open problem is to combine the two: causal representation learning, which means learning high-level causal variables directly from low-level observations.

In plain words. One field knows how to reason about “what happens if I do X?” but needs someone to hand it a tidy spreadsheet of variables first. The other field can read messy images and text but only learns which things tend to appear together. The paper asks how a machine could look at raw data, work out the meaningful “things” in it, and work out how those things cause one another.

The paper is a perspective / review paper, not an experimental one. It does three jobs:

  1. It is a primer on causal inference written for ML readers: structural causal models, interventions, counterfactuals.
  2. It proposes two guiding assumptions (inductive biases): the Independent Causal Mechanisms (ICM) principle and the Sparse Mechanism Shift (SMS) hypothesis.
  3. It reinterprets several ML topics through a causal lens: semi-supervised learning, robustness, adversarial examples, reinforcement learning, and scientific applications.

1. Motivation: why i.i.d. machine learning isn’t enough

1.1 The i.i.d. assumption

The idea. Most ML theory and practice assumes training and test data are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.): every example is drawn independently from one fixed distribution. Under that assumption, a model that fits the training data well will also do well on the test data. Modern ML is extremely good at this kind of large-scale pattern recognition.

In plain words. i.i.d. means “the exam questions come from the same pool as the practice questions.” If that holds, memorizing the patterns in the practice questions works. The trouble starts when the exam is written by someone else.

1.2 Issue 1: Robustness

The idea. When the test distribution differs from the training distribution (distribution shift or out-of-distribution data), accuracy can collapse. Blur, noise, rotation, a new camera angle, or a new background are enough. Data augmentation, pre-training, and self-supervision help, but mainly by widening the training distribution so that more situations count as “in distribution.” The authors argue that true out-of-distribution generalization needs a model in which shifts are understood as interventions on an underlying causal system.

In plain words. A model that learned “cows are on green grass” may fail to recognize a cow on a beach. Showing it more beaches helps, but it never learns why a cow is a cow. A causal model would understand that the background is a separate factor, and that changing it does not change what the animal is.

1.3 Issue 2: Reusable mechanisms

The idea. Animals do not relearn physics for every new task. If a model’s internal modules corresponded to real physical mechanisms (gravity, lighting, object shape), then a new environment would only require adapting the few modules that actually changed. Today’s networks are trained end-to-end as one monolithic block and are usually re-trained as a whole.

In plain words. When you move to a new city you don’t relearn how to walk, how gravity works, or what a door is. You only learn the new street layout. Current neural networks are more like someone who has to relearn everything from scratch after moving.

1.4 What animals learn from, and ML averages away

The idea. Interventions (acting on the world), domain shifts (changing environments), and temporal structure (things happening in sequence) are the richest sources of information for animals. Standard ML treats them as nuisances to be averaged out so the data looks i.i.d.

In plain words. A child learns by poking things and watching what happens. Standard ML throws away exactly that kind of information.

1.5 Why conditional probabilities aren’t enough: storks and babies

The idea. Across European regions, stork populations correlate with human birth rates. A statistical model can use this: \(P(\text{births} \mid \text{storks})\) really is predictive as long as nothing changes. But it cannot answer “If we released more storks, would births rise?” That question asks for \(P(\text{births} \mid do(\text{storks}))\), the distribution of births when we set the number of storks by intervention. Observational conditional probabilities cannot provide this on their own.

The central claim:

  • a conditional distribution tells you what to expect when you observe something;
  • a causal model tells you what happens when you act;
  • generalizing under shift requires the second.

In plain words. Seeing more storks in a region is a decent hint that the region has more babies (perhaps both are more common in rural areas). Releasing storks won’t make anyone pregnant. “Seeing” and “doing” are different questions, and data collected by passive watching usually only answers the first.

The \(do(\cdot)\) operator. \(P(Y \mid X = x)\) means “among cases where we happened to see \(X = x\), how is \(Y\) distributed?” \(P(Y \mid do(X = x))\) means “if we forced \(X\) to be \(x\) for everyone, how would \(Y\) be distributed?” The two coincide only when nothing else links \(X\) and \(Y\).

2. Levels of causal modeling

2.1 A hierarchy of models (Table I of the paper)

Model Predict i.i.d. Predict under shift / intervention Counterfactuals Physical insight Learn from data
Mechanistic / physical yes yes yes yes ?
Structural causal model yes yes yes ? ?
Causal graphical model yes yes no ? ?
Statistical model yes no no no yes

The idea. Models form a ladder. At the top, physical models (differential equations) can do everything but are rarely learnable from data. At the bottom, statistical models are easy to learn from data but can only predict in the i.i.d. setting. Each step down trades reasoning power for learnability. The paper’s agenda is to get the capabilities of the top rows with the learnability of the bottom row.

In plain words. A physics textbook explains everything but someone has to write it. A pattern-matcher learns itself from data but can’t explain or adapt. The goal is a model that learns itself and understands cause and effect.

2.2 The gold standard: physical models

The idea. A physical model is a system of differential equations,

\[\frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt} = f(\mathbf{x}), \qquad \mathbf{x}(t_0) = \mathbf{x}_0 .\]

Discretized in time it becomes an update rule:

\[\mathbf{x}(t + dt) = \mathbf{x}(t) + dt \cdot f(\mathbf{x}(t)).\]

This is inherently causal: the future is computed from the present. Such a model can predict the effect of any intervention (change a parameter and re-run the simulation), and its causal structure can be read off \(f\) by checking which components of \(\mathbf{x}\) affect which.

Limitations: it needs hand-derived mechanisms and known variables; it breaks down with unobserved confounders or with a choice of variables that doesn’t match the real causal structure; and it is rarely learnable directly from raw data.

In plain words. A physics simulation of a ball says exactly where the ball will be next, given where it is now. Want to know what happens on the Moon? Change gravity and run it again. The catch is that a scientist had to work out the equations, and for most real problems (a patient, an economy) nobody has them.

2.3 Four things we might want from a model

A. Predict in the i.i.d. setting. Idea: estimate \(P(Y \mid X)\) accurately. Statistical models are enough, and spurious correlations like storks–births are perfectly fine for this job. Plain words: if the world stays the same, any reliable pattern is useful, whether or not it is causal.

B. Predict under distribution shift. Idea: interventions change the joint distribution, so i.i.d. guarantees no longer apply. A model you can trust is one whose predictions survive changed conditions. Plain words: will the model still work at a new hospital, in a new season, with a new camera?

C. Answer counterfactuals. Idea: interventional and counterfactual queries lie on a continuum, written \(P(A \mid B, do(C))\). A counterfactual is the extreme case where the observed evidence \(B\) pins down all the noise terms (all the specifics of this particular case). Reinforcement learning Q-functions sit in between: they ask what return to expect if I take action \(a\) in state \(s\). Plain words: an interventional question is “what would happen to people like this if we gave the drug?” A counterfactual is “this specific patient didn’t get the drug and got worse; would they have recovered if they had?”

D. Learn from the data we have. Idea: data varies along two axes: observational vs. interventional (did we just watch, or did we act?), and hand-engineered vs. raw (tidy variables, or pixels?). Statistical models learn from raw observational data. Causal discovery usually needs data from multiple environments or interventions. Plain words: the easiest data to get (passively recorded, raw) is the hardest to learn causes from.

3. Causal models and inference

3.1 Reichenbach’s common cause principle

The idea. If two variables \(X\) and \(Y\) are statistically dependent, then some variable \(Z\) causally influences both and screens off the dependence: once you condition on \(Z\), \(X\) and \(Y\) become independent (\(X \perp\!\!\!\perp Y \mid Z\)). \(Z\) can be \(X\) itself (so \(X \to Y\)) or \(Y\) itself (so \(Y \to X\)).

So a dependence between \(X\) and \(Y\) has three possible explanations:

  1. \(X \to Y\) (storks cause births),
  2. \(Y \to X\) (births cause storks),
  3. \(X \leftarrow Z \to Y\) (a common cause, such as rurality, drives both).

Observational data alone cannot tell these apart. All three produce the same dependence. Telling them apart takes extra assumptions, interventions, or data from several environments.

In plain words. Correlation always has a causal explanation, but the correlation doesn’t tell you which one. Ice-cream sales and drownings rise together because summer drives both. Once you hold the season fixed, the link disappears. That is what “screening off” means.

3.2 Structural Causal Models (SCMs)

The idea. An SCM says each variable is produced by a deterministic function of its direct causes (its parents, \(\mathbf{PA}_i\)) plus its own random noise term \(U_i\):

\[X_i := f_i(\mathbf{PA}_i, U_i), \qquad i = 1, \dots, n,\]

where the noises \(U_1, \dots, U_n\) are jointly independent. The “:=” is an assignment, not an equation: it says \(X_i\) is computed from the right-hand side, and the reverse does not hold. Drawing an arrow from each parent to its child gives a directed acyclic graph (DAG).

In plain words. An SCM is a recipe. Each ingredient is made from earlier ingredients plus a bit of randomness specific to it. For example: fitness := f(genes, noise₁), exercise := g(fitness, noise₂), heart_health := h(fitness, exercise, noise₃). The noise covers everything about this particular case that the model doesn’t spell out.

3.3 Causal vs. entangled factorization

The idea. Any joint distribution can be broken into a product of conditional distributions in many ways. Two matter here:

  • Causal (disentangled) factorization: \[P(X_1, \dots, X_n) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} P(X_i \mid \mathbf{PA}_i).\] Each factor corresponds to exactly one structural assignment, one mechanism. This is the only factorization whose pieces line up with how the data was actually generated.

  • Entangled factorization (for example, the chain rule in an arbitrary order): \[P(X_1, \dots, X_n) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} P(X_i \mid X_{i+1}, \dots, X_n).\] It is an equally correct description of the joint distribution, but each factor mixes several mechanisms together.

Both describe the same observational distribution. The difference only appears when something changes, and the rest of the paper is built on that.

In plain words. You can describe a finished cake as a list of separate steps (make batter, bake, frost) or as one tangled description of “how frosting relates to batter given oven temperature.” Both describe the same cake. If you then switch to a different frosting, the step-by-step version needs one line changed, while the tangled version needs to be rewritten throughout.

3.4 Interventions: one model, many distributions

The idea. A statistical model describes one joint distribution. A causal model describes a whole family of distributions, one for each possible intervention. An intervention replaces one or more structural assignments and leaves the rest untouched. The paper distinguishes:

  • Hard intervention: set \(X_i\) to a constant (\(do(X_i = c)\)). Cut all arrows into \(X_i\), fix its value, and let its children respond as usual.
  • Soft intervention: change the mechanism \(f_i\) or the distribution of its noise, but keep the same parents. \(X_i\) still listens to its causes, just differently.
  • Uncertain intervention: we know something was intervened on, but not which mechanism.

In plain words. - Hard: “Everyone takes exactly 10 mg.” Whatever would normally decide the dose no longer matters. - Soft: “Doctors now prescribe a bit more than before.” The dose still depends on the patient, but the rule has shifted. - Uncertain: “The new hospital is different in some way we can’t pin down.”

3.5 Graphical models vs. SCMs

The idea. A graph with arrows is not automatically causal. The chains \(X_1 \to X_2 \to X_3\) and \(X_1 \leftarrow X_2 \leftarrow X_3\) imply exactly the same conditional independencies (\(X_1 \perp\!\!\!\perp X_3 \mid X_2\)). They are Markov equivalent: observational data cannot decide between them, so the arrow directions are arbitrary as far as that data is concerned. A graph becomes causal only when its arrows are claimed to hold under intervention.

Interventions Counterfactuals
Causal graphical model yes no
SCM yes yes

The SCM’s explicit noise terms \(U_i\) are what make counterfactuals possible. The standard three-step recipe:

  1. Abduction: from what actually happened, infer the noise values for this specific case.
  2. Action: change the model by applying the hypothetical intervention.
  3. Prediction: recompute the outcome using the same inferred noise.

In plain words. A causal graph tells you which way the influence flows, which is enough to answer “what happens on average if we do X?” A counterfactual about one specific person also needs that person’s individual quirks: their noise terms. The SCM keeps track of those quirks, so it can replay the same person’s story with one thing changed.

3.6 Confounders

The idea. A confounder is an unobserved common cause of two variables. It can create a spurious association where no causal link exists, or hide a real one. Many causal discovery methods assume there are no hidden confounders, and when that assumption is false they fail silently: they return a confident but wrong graph.

In plain words. People who carry lighters get lung cancer more often. Lighters don’t cause cancer; smoking causes both. If smoking isn’t recorded, an analysis can wrongly conclude that lighters are dangerous.

4. Independent causal mechanisms

4.1 The ICM principle

The idea. “The causal generative process of a system’s variables is composed of autonomous modules that do not inform or influence each other.” The principle makes two separate claims:

  • (a) No influence. Intervening on one mechanism \(P(X_i \mid \mathbf{PA}_i)\) leaves every other mechanism unchanged. Related terms: modularity, autonomy, invariance, separate intervenability.
  • (b) No information. Knowing the other mechanisms tells you nothing about a given mechanism. In algorithmic-information terms, the mechanisms’ shortest descriptions share no mutual information.

The idea has long roots in econometrics and philosophy of science: Haavelmo (1944), Simon’s invariance criterion, Aldrich (1989), Hoover (2008), Pearl (2009).

In plain words. Nature is built from separate, swappable parts. (a) Changing one part doesn’t break the others: replacing a car’s tyres doesn’t change how its engine works. (b) Studying one part tells you nothing about another: knowing the tyre brand gives no clue about engine design. Each mechanism is its own fact about the world.

4.2 Example: altitude and temperature

The idea. Weather-station data: altitude \(A\) causes average annual temperature \(T\).

  • Causal factorization: \(P(A, T) = P(A)\, P(T \mid A)\).
    • \(P(A)\) is the distribution of station altitudes, which depends on geography and varies by country.
    • \(P(T \mid A)\) is how temperature depends on altitude. That is physics, and it is the same everywhere.
    • Moving from Austria to Switzerland changes \(P(A)\) only.
  • Anticausal factorization: \(P(A, T) = P(T)\, P(A \mid T)\).
    • Both factors mix geography with physics, so changing the country shifts both.

The causal factorization transfers: in a new country you only need to re-estimate one module. This is the practical payoff of ICM for domain adaptation.

In plain words. Air gets colder as you go up a mountain at the same rate in every country. What differs between countries is how many mountains they have. If you store your knowledge as “how many mountains here” plus “how cold it gets with height,” you only need to update the first part when you travel. If you store it the other way round (“how warm it is here” plus “how high you probably are given the temperature”), both parts are wrong in the new country.

4.3 The Sparse Mechanism Shift (SMS) hypothesis

The idea. Small distribution changes tend to act sparsely or locally in the causal (disentangled) factorization: they affect one or a few mechanisms and rarely all of them at once.

  • In the causal factorization, a real-world change (new hospital, new camera, new country) touches one or a few factors \(P(X_i \mid \mathbf{PA}_i)\). The rest stay fixed, so adapting is cheap.
  • In an entangled factorization, the same change spreads across many terms. Every factor seems to move, and adapting looks expensive and unstructured.

Turned around, SMS becomes a learning signal. If the right representation is the one under which real shifts look sparse, then we can search for representations under which observed shifts are sparse. That gives a form of supervision that requires no labels.

In plain words. When something changes in the world, usually only one or two things actually change. A new lab installs a different microscope; patient biology stays the same. If your model of the world is organised the right way, that change shows up as one small edit. If it’s organised the wrong way, it shows up as a mess everywhere. So “which way of describing the data makes changes look small and local?” is a clue to the right description.

4.4 Causal discovery meets ML

The idea. Causal discovery means learning the causal graph from data. There are two main routes.

Route 1: conditional-independence (CI) tests. - Under the faithfulness assumption (every independence in the data comes from the graph structure and not from coincidental cancellations), CI tests recover the graph up to its Markov equivalence class: graphs with the same skeleton (the edges without directions) and the same immoralities (v-structures \(X \to Z \leftarrow Y\) where \(X\) and \(Y\) are not directly connected). - CI testing is hard with finite data and with continuous, high-dimensional conditioning sets. - With only two variables there are no non-trivial CI statements at all, so this route cannot tell \(X \to Y\) from \(Y \to X\).

Route 2: restrict the function class. - The general SCM for \(X \to Y\) is \(X := U\), \(Y := f(X, V)\). That is too flexible to identify direction. - The additive noise model restricts it: \(Y := f(X) + V\) with \(X \perp\!\!\!\perp V\). - Generically, this model cannot be fitted in the reverse direction (there is no \(g\) and noise \(\tilde V\) with \(X = g(Y) + \tilde V\) and \(Y \perp\!\!\!\perp \tilde V\)). That asymmetry reveals the causal direction. The exception is the linear-Gaussian case, where both directions fit equally well.

Multiple environments help. Shifts across environments, interventions, or non-stationary data reveal structure. Under SMS, the causal mechanisms stay invariant while other things move, and that invariance can be detected.

In plain words. - CI tests work like detective work: “if A and C stop being related once I know B, then B probably sits between them.” This only gets you so far and needs lots of data. - Additive noise models rely on a fingerprint. If \(Y\) is “a function of \(X\) plus independent random wobble,” then trying to explain \(X\) from \(Y\) leaves wobble that does depend on \(Y\), which gives away the wrong direction. (This fails if everything is linear and bell-curve shaped, because then the fingerprint vanishes.) - Data from several settings works like a controlled comparison: whatever stays stable across settings is likely a true mechanism.

5. Causal representation learning

5.1 The setting: causal variables aren’t given

The idea. Classical causal inference starts from a table of well-defined variables. Real inputs are pixels, waveforms, clinical notes: high-dimensional, low-level views in which the underlying causal variables are mixed together. Before any causal reasoning can happen, someone or something has to decide what the variables are. Figure 2 of the paper shows perceptual data as an entangled view of an unknown causal system.

In plain words. A camera doesn’t record “ball,” “table,” “gravity.” It records a million coloured dots. Causal tools need the ball and the table as named things. Causal representation learning is about getting from the dots to the things, choosing things that actually cause one another.

5.2 Problem formulation

The idea. Observations \(X\) are produced from a small number of latent causal variables \(S_1, \dots, S_n\) through a nonlinear map \(G\):

\[X = G(S_1, \dots, S_n), \qquad S_i := f_i(\mathbf{PA}_i, U_i), \qquad P(S_1, \dots, S_n) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} P(S_i \mid \mathbf{PA}_i).\]

The proposed architecture is an autoencoder with an SCM in the bottleneck:

  • an encoder \(q\) maps pixels \(X\) to independent noise variables \(U_1, \dots, U_n\);
  • the causal mechanisms \(f\) (an SCM) turn the noises into causal variables \(S_1, \dots, S_n\);
  • a decoder \(p\) maps the causal variables back to a reconstruction \(\hat X\).

Training aims for \(p \circ f \circ q \approx \mathrm{id}\) (the round trip reproduces the input).

The catch is identifiability. Without further assumptions, recovering the true \(U\) or \(S\) is ill-posed: many different latent descriptions reconstruct the data equally well. This is the same wall that limits unsupervised disentanglement (Locatello et al., 2019).

In plain words. Compress an image down to a few causal “knobs,” let those knobs influence each other according to learned cause-and-effect rules, then redraw the image from them. If the redrawing matches the original, the knobs capture what matters. The problem is that many different sets of knobs can redraw the image perfectly well, and nothing in the image alone forces the machine to pick the true causal ones. Extra hints are needed, such as data from changing environments.

5.3 SMS in pixel space

The idea. Figure 3 of the paper: one finger moves and an object falls. In pixel space the change is dense: thousands of pixel values change, all entangled. In the causal model the change is sparse: one variable (the finger position) is intervened on and its children (the object’s position) follow. A good causal representation is one in which real-world changes look this sparse.

In plain words. To a camera, a cup falling off a table changes almost the whole picture. To a person, only one thing happened: the cup was nudged and gravity did the rest. A good representation sees it the way the person does.

5.4 Three problems for causal representation learning

  1. Learning disentangled representations. Idea: find an encoder whose latent variables behave like independent causal mechanisms: they factorize as \(\prod_i P(S_i \mid \mathbf{PA}_i)\) and respond sparsely to shifts. SMS and multi-environment data supply the supervision that is otherwise missing. Plain words: learn separate knobs for separate real-world factors, so turning one doesn’t secretly turn others.

  2. Learning transferable mechanisms. Idea: with limited data and compute, reuse modules that mirror the modularity of the world. A mechanism learned in one task (for example, how lighting affects appearance) should plug into the next task. Plain words: build a toolkit of reusable parts instead of a new custom machine for every job.

  3. Learning interventional world models and reasoning. Idea: representations should support acting: intervening, planning, and asking counterfactual “what if I had…?” questions. Statistical dependence isn’t enough for this. It connects to the view of thinking as acting in an imagined space. Plain words: the model should let an agent imagine “if I push this, then that happens” before doing it, the way people rehearse actions in their heads.

6. Implications for machine learning

6.1 Semi-supervised learning: direction matters

The idea. Semi-supervised learning (SSL) uses unlabeled inputs \(X\) to improve a predictor of \(Y\). Whether it can help depends on causal direction.

  • Causal direction, \(X \to Y\): \(P(X, Y) = P(X)\, P(Y \mid X)\). By ICM (no information), \(P(X)\) tells you nothing about \(P(Y \mid X)\), so unlabeled \(X\) shouldn’t help. Example: predicting a protein from its mRNA sequence.
  • Anticausal direction, \(Y \to X\): \(P(X, Y) = P(Y)\, P(X \mid Y)\). Now \(P(X)\) and \(P(Y \mid X)\) are coupled, so unlabeled \(X\) can help, which is where the usual cluster and low-density assumptions apply. Example: classifying a handwritten digit from its image, where the digit’s identity causes the pixels.

A meta-analysis cited by the authors found that SSL gains were concentrated in anticausal tasks. That is a concrete, testable prediction of ICM.

In plain words. If the label causes the input (the digit “7” causes the ink pattern), then piles of unlabeled images naturally fall into clusters, one per digit, and those clusters help you draw boundaries. If the input causes the label (the gene sequence determines the protein), then the way inputs are spread out says nothing about the rule that maps them to outputs, so extra unlabeled inputs don’t help.

6.2 Adversarial examples

The idea. Adversarial examples are a deliberate violation of i.i.d.: tiny, carefully chosen pixel changes cause large changes in the prediction. A classifier that works in the anticausal direction (image → label) ignores how images are actually generated. Models that approximate the causal generative process should be harder to fool. One proposed defense is analysis by synthesis: check whether the input is consistent with a generative model of the predicted class.

In plain words. Adversarial attacks find blind spots by nudging pixels in ways no real-world process would. A model that understands how a real “panda” image comes about would notice that the tampered image could not have been produced that way, so it would be harder to trick. Analysis by synthesis means asking “if this really were a gibbon, would it look like this?”

6.3 Out-of-distribution robustness

The idea. The risk (expected loss) of predictor \(f\) in environment \(e\) is

\[R^{P^e}(f) = \mathbb{E}_{(X, Y) \sim P^e}\big[\ell(f(X), Y)\big].\]

A robust predictor minimizes the worst-case risk over a set of environments \(\mathcal{E}\):

\[f^\star = \arg\min_f \max_{e \in \mathcal{E}} R^{P^e}(f).\]

If \(\mathcal{E}\) is generated by interventions on variables other than the target, the causal predictor (which uses only the direct causes of \(Y\)) solves this min-max problem. The difficulty is that we never observe every \(e \in \mathcal{E}\), so estimation error on unseen environments can be large. Causal structure is what makes extrapolation to unseen environments possible.

In plain words. Instead of being best on average, be never terrible, even in the worst setting you might face. If settings differ only by changes to things other than the outcome’s true causes, a model that relies only on the true causes performs equally well everywhere. Shortcuts may look good in some settings but fail badly in others.

6.4 Re-reading today’s tricks through a causal lens

The idea. - Pre-training approximates the min-max by covering a broader training distribution, which amounts to including more environments implicitly. - Data augmentation encodes assumed invariances (rotation, crop, colour) as simulated interventions on nuisance variables. - Self-supervision learns about \(P(X)\), which, following §6.1, helps most in anticausal settings.

These are heuristic approximations to robust causal learning. The paper asks that we understand which intervention each trick protects against, rather than stacking tricks blindly.

In plain words. Rotating training images is a way of telling the model “rotation doesn’t change what this is.” That works, but only for the changes you thought to simulate. A causal view would make the list of protected changes explicit.

6.5 Reinforcement learning

The idea. RL is arguably closer to causality than supervised learning, because on-policy learning estimates interventional quantities by actually acting. Off-policy and offline RL (learning from someone else’s logged actions) reintroduce confounding: the logged actions were chosen for reasons that may also affect outcomes. World models and counterfactual reasoning remain open frontiers.

In plain words. An agent that tries things and sees the results is running experiments. An agent that learns only from watching others is back to correlations, and those can mislead: maybe the expert only took that action when things were already going well.

6.6 Science and medicine

The idea. - Personalized medicine: observational clinical data can mislead treatment-effect estimates because of confounding (for example, sicker patients get more aggressive treatment). - Covid-19 and Simpson’s paradox: early in the pandemic, Italy’s overall case-fatality rate was higher than China’s, yet within every age group Italy’s rate was lower. The reversal came from age structure: Italy’s cases skewed much older. Causal reasoning about age as a variable resolves the apparent contradiction. - Exoplanets and half-sibling regression: in Kepler telescope data, stars observed by the same instrument share instrument noise but cannot share each other’s astrophysical signals. Predicting one star’s light curve from many other stars captures only the shared noise. Subtracting that prediction removes the noise and leaves the real signal. This produced 36 planet candidates, 21 later validated. - Physics simulation and learning dynamics from data.

In plain words. - Simpson’s paradox: a trend can reverse when you split data into groups. One country can do better in every age bracket and still look worse overall because it has more elderly patients. - Half-sibling regression: if a hundred unrelated stars all flicker at the same moment, the telescope is probably to blame and not the stars. Subtract the shared flicker and what remains is the star’s own behaviour, which can include a planet passing in front of it.

6.7 Multi-task and continual learning

The idea. Humans reuse one generative model of the world across many tasks. A shared causal model with SMS-compliant components (components whose changes are sparse) would let a system adapt to a new task by changing a few modules, and learn new mechanisms without forgetting old ones. A giant end-to-end model, by contrast, has to refit everything.

In plain words. Learning to drive a truck after a car means adjusting a few skills, not forgetting how to drive. Modular causal models would allow the same for machines, and avoid “catastrophic forgetting,” where learning something new erases something old.

7. Open problems, critique, and clinical relevance

7.1 Open problems named by the authors

  1. Non-linear causal relations at scale. When can they be learned? Which training frameworks scale? Do causal modules actually transfer better than non-causal ones? Plain words: we don’t yet know how to do this on large, realistic problems, or how much it really helps.

  2. Learning causal variables. Disentangled representations are fixed-size vectors with an arbitrary ordering, so they can’t handle a changing number of objects. We need modular, task-dependent representations and a theory of identifiability, meaning conditions under which the true variables can be recovered. Plain words: a scene can contain two objects or twenty, and current methods assume a fixed number of slots.

  3. Biases of existing deep learning. Scale, augmentation and self-supervision all improve robustness, but their effects are tangled together. Methods should be tested against specific interventions to build a taxonomy of inductive biases. Plain words: we know these tricks help but not exactly against what. Test each one against specific kinds of change.

  4. Causally correct models of world and agent. Real RL rarely comes with clean, abstract states. Causal variables and graphs need to be derived from pixels, and models should cover both the agent and the environment for model-based RL. Plain words: a robot has to work out from camera images what the meaningful things are, including itself.

7.2 Critical appraisal

Strengths - Unifies two communities with a shared vocabulary (SCMs ↔︎ representations). - ICM and SMS are concrete, testable inductive biases, not vague appeals to “causality.” - Reinterprets familiar phenomena (SSL, adversarial examples, augmentation) in a way that actually explains them. - Agenda-setting: much of the later identifiable-CRL literature traces back to it.

Limitations - A position paper: no new experiments or benchmarks. - Identifiability is acknowledged but not solved. Without some supervision, “causal variables” may be unrecoverable. - SMS is a hypothesis. When do real shifts actually look sparse? - Little guidance on evaluation: how would we know a learned variable is causal? - The scaling arguments predate the foundation-model era. Does scale already buy much of the promised robustness?

7.3 Why this matters for clinical data

The idea. - Distribution shift is the default. Multi-site networks (for example PCORnet or OMOP-based federations) are effectively many environments, with different populations, coding practices, and EHR vendors. Under SMS, a new site should change a few mechanisms (case mix, documentation habits) and not the underlying physiology. Robust models should keep those apart. - Variables aren’t given here either. Phenotypes, disease stages, and severity are latent variables we construct from codes, labs, and notes. Phenotyping and sub-phenotyping are, in effect, representation learning problems.

Discussion prompt: in our own pipelines, which “variables” are we treating as given that are really learned, and possibly not causal? Which of our learned phenotypes would survive an intervention?

In plain words. Every hospital is a slightly different world, but the human body works the same way in all of them. A good model should separate “how this hospital records things” from “how the disease works.” And “has diabetes” in a dataset is often not a measured fact. It is a definition someone built from billing codes and lab values, so it deserves the same scrutiny as any learned representation.

8. Key takeaways

  1. Seeing vs. doing. Statistical models answer “what will I observe?” Causal models answer “what happens if I act?” Generalizing under shift needs the second.
  2. Modularity and sparse change. The ICM principle says causal mechanisms are modular and independent. The SMS hypothesis says real-world changes touch only a few of them.
  3. Learning the variables. Causal inference assumes the variables are given. Causal representation learning is about learning them from raw data, using SMS and multi-environment data as supervision.
  4. A new lens on old puzzles. Familiar ML phenomena (SSL working only sometimes, adversarial fragility, the value of augmentation) make more sense from a causal point of view.

9. Glossary

Term Precise meaning Plain meaning
i.i.d. Independent and identically distributed: all samples are drawn independently from one fixed distribution. Test data looks like training data.
Distribution shift / OOD Test distribution differs from training distribution. The world changed after you trained.
Intervention, \(do(X = x)\) Replace the mechanism for \(X\) (e.g., fix its value) and leave all others intact. Actively setting something, as opposed to watching it.
Hard / soft / uncertain intervention Fix to a constant / alter the mechanism but keep parents / unknown target. Force it / nudge it / something changed, not sure what.
Counterfactual Query about what would have happened to a specific unit under a different action, given what actually happened. “What if this person had done otherwise?”
SCM Set of assignments \(X_i := f_i(\mathbf{PA}_i, U_i)\) with independent noises. A recipe saying how each variable is produced.
Parents, \(\mathbf{PA}_i\) Direct causes of \(X_i\) in the causal graph. The things that directly affect \(X_i\).
Noise term, \(U_i\) Unobserved, independent source of variation for \(X_i\). Everything about this case the model doesn’t spell out.
DAG Directed acyclic graph: arrows, no cycles. A flowchart of causes with no loops.
Causal (disentangled) factorization \(\prod_i P(X_i \mid \mathbf{PA}_i)\); one factor per mechanism. Knowledge stored as separate, swappable parts.
Entangled factorization Any other valid factorization of the joint; factors mix mechanisms. Knowledge stored as one tangled lump.
Mechanism A single conditional \(P(X_i \mid \mathbf{PA}_i)\) / assignment \(f_i\). One cause-and-effect rule.
Common cause principle Dependence implies a common cause that screens it off (possibly \(X\) or \(Y\) itself). Every correlation has a causal story behind it.
Screening off \(X \perp\!\!\!\perp Y \mid Z\). Once you know \(Z\), \(X\) tells you nothing more about \(Y\).
Confounder Unobserved common cause of two variables. A hidden third factor creating a fake link.
Markov equivalence Different DAGs implying the same conditional independencies. Graphs that observational data can’t tell apart.
Markov equivalence class All DAGs sharing a skeleton and immoralities. The best observational data alone can narrow things down to.
Immorality / v-structure \(X \to Z \leftarrow Y\) with \(X, Y\) not adjacent. Two separate causes of a shared effect.
Faithfulness Every independence in the data is implied by the graph. No coincidental cancellations hide real links.
Additive noise model \(Y := f(X) + V\), \(X \perp\!\!\!\perp V\). Effect = function of cause + independent wobble; reveals direction.
ICM principle Mechanisms neither influence nor inform each other. Nature is built from independent, swappable parts.
SMS hypothesis Real distribution shifts change few mechanisms in the causal factorization. When the world changes, usually only one or two things change.
Causal representation learning Learning latent causal variables (and their mechanisms) from raw observations. Going from pixels to the meaningful things and how they affect each other.
Identifiability Whether the true latent variables can be uniquely recovered from data (up to trivial transformations). Whether the data contains enough clues to find the real answer.
Disentangled representation Latents that correspond to separate generative factors. One knob per real-world factor.
Causal / anticausal learning Predicting effect from cause (\(X \to Y\)) vs. cause from effect (\(Y \to X\)). Forward along the arrow vs. backward against it.
Analysis by synthesis Classify by checking consistency with a generative model of each class. “Could this class really have produced this image?”
Min-max (worst-case) risk \(\min_f \max_{e} R^{P^e}(f)\). Be never terrible, rather than best on average.
Simpson’s paradox A trend in aggregate data reverses within every subgroup. Splitting into groups can flip the story.
Half-sibling regression Remove shared noise by regressing a target on variables sharing only the noise. Subtract what everything has in common to reveal what’s unique.
